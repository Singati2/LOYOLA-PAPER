## 0. Scope and method

I read every statement and proof in Sections 2 to 5 of main.tex, Supplementary Sections S5 and S7, LEAN_VERIFICATION.md and all Lean sources in formal/. I checked the central claims with my own scripts. They do not import any checking code from the repository. Profiles and q-histograms use exact rationals. Irrational index values use 50 to 60 digit mpmath arithmetic, the one GA coincidence found was confirmed symbolically in sympy, and the five crossings were certified with mpmath interval arithmetic. ## 2. Ratio-plane theorem and first collision orders

**Theorem 3.5(i).** On Π, Eq. (3.4) with β = −2α removes the degree-sum factor, so the value is Σ_q n_q ((1−q²)/4)^α e^{γq}. The "if and only if" holds. For the measure-zero clause the proof restricts to α = 0, obtains a nonzero exponential sum in γ with distinct exponents, and concludes that the difference is a nonzero real-analytic function on the plane. Its zero set is then null. The logic is complete. **Correct.**

**Theorem 3.5(ii).** All imbalances are rational, so for algebraic γ ≠ 0 the exponents γq are distinct algebraic numbers. Lindemann–Weierstrass in its linear-independence form, applied with integer coefficients, gives the claim. The real-γ clause follows from Prop 3.11 (at most k−1 zeros for each pair, and finitely many pairs). Excluding γ = 0 is necessary, because at γ = 0 all graphs of equal size agree. **Correct.**

**Theorem 3.5(iii).** The pendant-path step changes each profile by −(1,d) + (2,d) + (L−1)(2,2) + (1,2). The L = 1 and d = 2 edge cases come out right: (L−1)(2,2) vanishes, and (2,d) becomes (2,2). Both profiles therefore shift by the same vector, the histograms shift by the same q-multiset, and the maximum degree is preserved because it is at least 2. I rebuilt the two order-13 trees and the two order-16 chemical trees from the verbal descriptions. Each has the stated order (13 and 16), the stated profile, the stated common histogram {0¹, ⅕², ⅓¹, ½³, (5/7)⁵} and {0³, ⅓⁴, (3/5)⁸} respectively, and a leaf adjacent to a vertex of degree 3 or 4 respectively. **Correct.**

**Corollary 3.6.** The relation ρ = (1−q)/(1+q) is a bijection between [0,1) and (0,1]. Every listed index (GA, AG, SDD, ISDD and its exponential, exponential GA, and the degree-ratio Sombor index) depends on an edge only through d_u/d_v. **Correct.**

**Remark 3.7.** The map q ↦ (1−q²)/4 is strictly decreasing on [0,1), so the bases are distinct and the difference is a nonzero exponential sum in α with at most k−1 zeros. **Correct.** The quantifier is per pair, however. For each pair the exceptional α are finitely many, but no single α is shown to work for all pairs, and at rational α collisions do occur (at α = 1 the value is a rational linear functional of the histogram). The pure-γ statement is uniform over all algebraic γ ≠ 0, which is exactly the contrast the remark draws. *Optional wording* for line 641-642: "... an exponential sum in α with distinct bases, which separates any two given distinct histograms at all but finitely many α, and hence all of them simultaneously at almost every α."

**Prop 3.8 and Remark 3.9.** For rational α and β the coefficients are algebraic and Lindemann–Weierstrass applies. The counterexample LO(K_4;0,0,ln 4) = 6 = 3e^{(ln 4)/2} = LO(K_{1,3};0,0,ln 4) holds. It is a genuine instance of a transcendental γ destroying a parent distinction, since M_{0,0} = m separates 6 from 3. **Correct.**

**Remark 3.10.** The best a point of Π can do is the number of histograms. Randić beats it at every order from 13 to 17 (570 > 566, 1095 > 1090, 2100 > 2093, 4007 > 3996, 7604 > 7582), and a generic point such as (0.3, 0.2, 0) separates all 4069 profiles at order 16. Among all trees GA merges distinct histograms for the first time at order 17, with exactly one merge. That merge is precisely the paper's pair, and the GA difference is 0 symbolically: 10√5/3 + 4 = 4√5/3 + 4 + 2√5. Among Δ ≤ 4 trees GA merges nothing up to order 17. **Correct.**

## 3. Order exchanges and the five-crossing certificate

**Prop 3.11.** Substituting x = e^γ turns D into a generalised polynomial with distinct real exponents. Laguerre's rule then bounds the positive zeros, counted with multiplicity, by V, with parity V. The six possible imbalances for Δ ≤ 4 are {0, 1/7, 1/5, 1/3, 1/2, 3/5}, which I checked over the ten pairs. This holds for all graphs with Δ ≤ 4, not only trees. **Correct.**

**Remark 3.12 (sharpness).** Both trees have order 12 and size 11, and their profiles satisfy the tree identities. At (α,β) = (−1,0) the class coefficients, ordered by q, are
(1/11: −1/30), (1/7: 1/12), (1/5: 1/24), (1/3: −1/2), (1/2: 7/12), (3/5: 1/2), (2/3: −4/5), (5/7: 1/6),
which gives eight distinct imbalances and V = 5. Bisection at 60 digits gives roots γ ≈ −17.1709, −11.9606, −2.02601, 2.73446, 31.1333. Each was certified by a strict sign change of D on [root − 10⁻⁶, root + 10⁻⁶], evaluated in 50-digit interval arithmetic. With V = 5 these are all the zeros, and each is simple. The paper's rounded values (−17.2, −12.0, −2.0, 2.7, 31.1) are right. **Correct.**

The open question in the remark (five exchanges with Δ ≤ 4) is plausibly open as stated. A grid search over all pairs of chemical-tree profiles of orders 3 to 12, with (α,β) ∈ [−3,3]² in steps of 0.5, found no pair with five sign changes. A random search over 40,000 small integer difference vectors with |Δm_ij| ≤ 6 and (α,β) ∈ [−6,6]² found at most three. Real coefficient vectors with five zeros on the six Δ ≤ 4 exponents do exist, but they need coefficient ratios of order 10² to 10⁶ (for example roots at γ = ±30, ±10, 0 need ratios near 1 : 113 : 227 : 134 : 20 : 0.94). If the authors want to add one sentence, the question is a realisability question for large graphs. No change is required.

## 5. Discrimination

**Prop 4.1 and the octane floor.** My SMILES parser gives 16 distinct count vectors among the 18 octanes, and the two collision classes match Table 4. From octane_data.py the within-class sums of squares are 18/25 and 441/200, so **SSE = 117/40 = 2.925 °C² exactly, and RMSE = √(2.925/18) = 0.40311 °C**. The logic is sound. Any predictor that is a function of the count vector is constant on each class, and on a class the mean minimises the squared error. The text (lines 903-907) already restricts the bound to a single fixed predictor and excludes cross-validated errors. **Correct.**

*Optional wording, line 888.* "carries a nonzero irreducible error on this benchmark" could read "carries a nonzero in-sample error on this benchmark (for T_B at least 2.925 °C² in total squared error)". *Line 1571:* "differ by up to 1.2 °C and 2.1 °C" is better written as "differ by 1.2 °C and 2.1 °C", since each pair has a single difference.

**Remark 4.2 (rem:countdim).** I checked the identities m₁₁ = 0, Σm = n−1 and Σ(1/i+1/j)m = n exactly on every Δ ≤ 4 tree of orders 3 to 17, with no violations. All 100 rows of bp_data.csv satisfy them too. Subtracting the two identities gives Σ(1/i + 1/j − 1)m = 1, which does not depend on n, so the bounds 7 (fixed n) and 8 (n varying) are right. The attained ranks reproduce: 7 for the 18 octanes, 7 for the 34 decanes and 8 for the 100 pooled alkanes. I also asked whether 7 is the true dimension at a fixed order. The affine dimension of the set of all Δ ≤ 4 tree count vectors of order n is 0, 1, 2, 4, 6 for n = 3 to 7, and exactly 7 for every n from 8 to 17. The pool over n = 3 to 17 has dimension 8. So no further linear constraint exists for n ≥ 8 in the checked range, and the bound is sharp there. **Correct.** *Optional wording*, lines 913-915: "The count vectors of such trees therefore lie in an affine subspace of dimension at most 7 at fixed n and at most 8 when n varies, since subtracting the two identities gives Σ(1/i+1/j−1)m_ij = 1 for every n. For each order 8 ≤ n ≤ 17 the trees attain dimension 7 (exhaustive check)."

**Prop 4.3 and the weak-discrimination sentence.** The z_ij are distinct, so a generic direction separates the exponents, and the difference is a nonzero real-analytic function with a null zero set. The countable-union step works because count vectors with finite support form a countable set. Hence the exceptional θ form a null set, and almost every θ is weakly discriminating over all graphs simultaneously. **Correct.**

**Example 4.4.** The difference vector satisfies Σ Δm = 0 and Σ(1/i+1/j)Δm = 0, consistent with two trees of the same order. D(0) = 0, D(1) = 0.225538 and D(10⁻⁶) = 2.40127 × 10⁻⁷, both from the displayed formula and directly from the count difference. **Correct.**

**Section 4.2, Table 2 and Supplementary S5.** Every count I recomputed matches: order-10 floor 79 (Δ ≤ 4: 49); χ at 77/79 from order 10 (also 147/152 and 281/294 at orders 11 and 12); Randić 570 at 13, 1095/1100 at 14, and 1191/1194 at order 17 for Δ ≤ 4; at order 16, (0,½,0) 3475, (0,⅓,0) 3476, (0,−½,0) 3476, (½,0,0) 3940, (−½,0,0) 4007, (⅓,0,0) 4009, all 4069 at γ = 1 except (½,0,1) 4067 and (−½,0,1) 4068, GA 3996 at both γ, and (0.3,0.2,0) 4069; and Supplementary Table S4 for orders 7 to 12. **Correct.**

The first order with two distinct degree-pair profiles sharing a q-histogram is 13 (4 such histogram classes; 10 at order 14, 26 at order 15, 69 at order 16). Among trees with Δ ≤ 4 the first is 16 (one class). This agrees with Theorem 3.5(iii) and Remark 3.10.

### 2. Classification of the central results

Key: (a) established mathematics applied to LOYOLA; (b) a new specialization or interpretation; (c) potentially original, needing careful literature qualification.

**Lemma 3.1, affine independence of (1, ln ij, ln(i+j), q_ij).** (b). It is a 4 × 4 determinant computation (the decisive fact is 3^8 ≠ 2^13). It shows that γ is a genuine functional direction, not a reparametrisation of (α, β), for Δ ≥ 3. Correct and useful, but elementary. In Rada's linear framework every BID index is a vector in the finite-dimensional weight space, so the lemma amounts to saying that the curve of weight vectors is not contained in the image of the parent family up to scale.

**Theorem 3.3, convexity of log LO, gradient as mean and Hessian as covariance.** (a). Since LO_G(θ) = Σ exp(θ·z_uv), log LO is a log-sum-exp of linear functions. Convexity, ∇ = E_θ[z], ∇² = Cov_θ(z), and the strict convexity criterion (features not in a proper affine subspace) are textbook facts about log partition functions of finite exponential families (Brown 1986, already cited). The genuinely LOYOLA-specific parts are the reading of ∂_γ log LO as a weighted mean imbalance and the identification of semiregular bipartite graphs as the affine case. Corollary 5.5 (Cauchy–Schwarz interpolation) is the midpoint case and the paper says so. Interpolation inequalities in the exponent for variable indices appear in Sigarreta 2021 (abstract level). That paper is not cited.

**Proposition 3.13, per-graph identifiability (rank of the Hessian equals the affine dimension A(G)).** (a) in substance: this is the minimal representation statement for an exponential family. Part (ii), the bound A(G) ≤ min(|R(G)| − 1, r(G), 3) from the ratio-plane factorisation, and Remark 3.14 (the only failing set in P_7 is {(1,7),(2,6),(3,5),(4,4)}) are (b).

**Theorem 3.5, ratio-plane theorem.**
* Part (i), the dependence on the q-histogram on Π = {β = −2α}. (b). It is an immediate consequence of the identity 4ij/(i+j)² = 1 − q², i.e. of the fact that (ij)^α (i+j)^{−2α} is a function of the degree ratio. That GA, AG, SDD and ISDD depend only on d_u/d_v is folklore. The new element is naming the plane inside the two-parameter Gutman–Milovanović family.
* Part (ii), exact separation of q-histograms at every algebraic γ ≠ 0. (a). It is Rada 2019 Theorem 3.4 applied to the edge function φ = γq, whose equal-weight classes are the q-classes. The paper states this at line 617. The real-γ clause (finitely many exceptional γ on a finite set) is the standard zero count for exponential sums.
* Part (iii), colliding families at every order n ≥ 13, and n ≥ 16 for Δ ≤ 4, with minimality by enumeration. (c), with qualification. The propagation step (attach the same pendant path to both trees) is Rada 2026 Section 3.1, used there for equal profiles; the paper now cites this at line 584. What I could not find anywhere is the determination of the first orders, 13 and 16, at which distinct profiles share a degree-ratio multiset, together with explicit witnesses. I confirmed the numbers independently.

**Corollary 3.6, degree-ratio indices.** (b). The statement that every Σ f(ρ_uv) depends only on the q-histogram is one line. Its value lies in combining it with part (iii): a single explicit pair of trees at order 13 (and at order 16 among chemical trees) defeats GA, AG, SDD, ISDD, exponential GA, the exponential ISDD, the diminished Sombor index and the whole degree-ratio Sombor cluster at once. None of the papers introducing those indices records this. This combination is the paper's most useful contribution to the discrimination literature.

**Remark 3.7 and the question whether the parent already reaches the same resolution limit.** It does, and the paper says so correctly. Two separate facts settle it.
1. On Π itself, at γ = 0, the parent points (α, −2α, 0) give Σ_q n_q ((1 − q²)/4)^α. Distinct q in [0,1) give distinct bases, so this is an exponential sum in α with distinct exponents. Two distinct histograms therefore receive different values for all but finitely many α. The parent reaches the q-histogram limit generically on Π without γ.
2. Off Π the parent does strictly better. Proposition 4.3 needs only the injectivity of (i,j) ↦ (ln ij, ln(i+j)), so for almost every (α, β) the parent M_{α,β} separates any two distinct degree-pair profiles, which is the absolute floor for every vertex-degree-based index (Proposition 4.1). The paper concedes this at lines 955 to 964 and in Section 4.2 (LO(0.3, 0.2, 0) separates all 4069 profiles at order 16).

Consequently γ adds nothing to resolution in the generic sense. What it adds is narrower: (i) a statement valid at every algebraic γ ≠ 0 rather than off an unspecified finite exceptional set (the parent at a fixed rational α has no such guarantee; GA = 2LO(1/2, −1, 0) merges two histograms at order 17); and (ii) Proposition 3.8, that at rational (α, β) an algebraic γ ≠ 0 never destroys a parent distinction and may restore distinctions lost at classical exponents (Example 4.4 for χ, enumeration for M_{0,1/2}, M_{0,1/3}). Both are Lindemann–Weierstrass applications, so (a) in method and (b) in content. Remark 3.7's own sentence "The limit thus belongs to the dependence on the degree ratio, not to γ" is accurate. The title, however, attaches the resolution limit to degree imbalance in a way the body does not support (see Section 4 below).

**Proposition 3.11, at most V ≤ r − 1 order exchanges along γ; at most five for Δ ≤ 4; sharpness for Δ = 6.** (a) in method (Laguerre's extension of Descartes' rule of signs) and (c) as an application: I know of no use of the rule of signs to bound order exchanges of a parametric degree-based index, and the earlier audit's searches found none. The sharp Δ = 6 example is a nice touch; whether five crossings occur for Δ ≤ 4 is honestly left open.

**Proposition 4.1 (degree-pair ceiling) and the collision error floor.** (a). Equal edge-type vectors imply equal values of every BID index; this is the starting point of Deutsch and Klavžar 2015 (equal M-polynomial) and of Rada 2026 Section 3.1. The octane collision pairs are well known to anyone who has tabulated m_ij for the octanes. The floor on the sum of squared errors is the within-class sum of squares, an elementary ANOVA fact; the number 2.925 °C² is a new datum but not a new result. Deutsch and Klavžar 2015 should be cited beside Rada 2026 for the ceiling.

**Proposition 4.3 (generic discrimination).** (a)/(b). Real-analytic nonvanishing plus measure-zero zero sets; the paper rightly states that it is inherited from the parent and uses the weak discrimination terminology of Rada, Rodríguez and Sigarreta 2022. The weak-discrimination reading over all classes at once is a tidy (b).

**Remark 4.2 (dimension of the counts).** (a). The relation Σ (1/i + 1/j) m_ij = n is the handshake identity regrouped by edge type, and m_11 = 0 for trees of order at least 3. Linear relations of this kind among the m_ij of chemical trees are routine in the BID literature (the bounds of Vukičević and Đurđević 2011 are built on such systems; I verified that paper's bibliographic data, not its exact relations). The rank statements for the data sets are (b).

**Section 5 termwise bounds (Propositions 5.4, 5.6, 5.7, 5.8, Corollaries 5.5 and 5.9).** (a). Each is a sum of per-edge inequalities. At γ = 0 they are the Gutman–Milovanović bounds the paper cites (Molina et al. 2025, Granados et al. 2025, Portilla et al. 2025). The ratio bound min ψ/h · I_h ≤ LO ≤ max ψ/h · I_h is the standard comparison device of BID theory. Inequalities between exponential indices in the same spirit are in Carballosa, Quintana, Rodríguez and Sigarreta 2023, not cited. The equality-case bookkeeping for γ > 0 is a correct (b) but minor.

**Proposition 5.2 (path and star extremal for LO(0,0,γ)).** (b). The star half is the normal-cone condition of Rada 2026 Section 4.2; the path half needs a short argument because e^{γ/3} > e^{γ/5}, which the paper supplies and compares with Cruz and Rada 2019. Liu et al. 2024 does not apply because e^{γq} is not monotone in each degree. This is correctly attributed.

### 3. Strongest defensible contribution

The single strongest defensible mathematical contribution is the exact location of the degree-ratio obstruction among trees: the smallest trees with distinct degree-pair profiles and equal degree-ratio multisets have order 13 (order 16 for chemical trees), with explicit witnesses and a propagation to every larger order. Combined with the elementary fact that every index of the form Σ f(d_u/d_v) factors through the q-histogram, this yields one explicit obstruction for GA, AG, SDD, ISDD, the exponential GA and ISDD indices, the diminished Sombor index and the degree-ratio Sombor indices simultaneously. The minimality is computer-assisted but easily reproduced, and I reproduced it. The Laguerre bound on order exchanges along γ is a modest second item.

How a skeptical MATCH referee would summarise it:

> The paper introduces a three-parameter exponential extension of the Gutman–Milovanović family. Its convexity, identifiability and generic discrimination results are standard exponential-family and real-analytic facts, and its separation result for the pure-γ points is Rada's 2019 Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem applied to the edge function γq. The authors concede that the parent family already separates distinct degree-ratio histograms on the ratio plane, and distinct degree-pair profiles off it, for almost all exponents, so γ adds no generic discriminating power. The genuinely new content is combinatorial: the first tree orders, 13 in general and 16 for chemical trees, at which distinct degree-pair profiles share a degree-ratio histogram, with explicit witnesses, which shows at once that every degree-ratio index (GA, AG, SDD, ISDD, the diminished and degree-ratio Sombor indices) fails to separate them. Together with a rule-of-signs bound on order exchanges along γ, this is a short but publishable note on degree-ratio indices; the family itself is a convenient vehicle rather than the source of the result. The predictive analysis is honest and negative.
